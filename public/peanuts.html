Peanuts

About half of the peanuts eaten in the United States are grown in Georgia, and most of that crop comes out of the sandy soils south and west of Macon. A peanut is technically a legume that grows underground, which is why fields at harvest look like nothing happened above the surface until the diggers roll through.

How the crop works

Peanuts are planted in April and May once soil temperatures stay above 65°F. The plants flower above ground; after fertilization the flower stalks bend downward and push into the soil, where the peanut pods form and mature. A farmer watches the pods, not the plant, to decide when to dig — usually 130 to 150 days after planting. Timing is unforgiving. A crop dug a week early loses yield; a week late and pods start dropping off the vine.

Harvest

Harvest is a two-step process. A digger runs under the plants and flips them upside down so the pods air-dry in the sun for a few days. Then a combine picks up the windrows, separates the pods from the vines, and blows the peanuts into a trailer. Middle Georgia's peanut harvest runs from mid-September through late October, and during those weeks buying points from Fort Valley to Plains run around the clock.

Peanut facts

State share
Georgia grows about 50% of the US peanut crop.
Main type
Runners — the variety used for most peanut butter.
Yield
Roughly 4,500 pounds per acre in an average year.
Rotation partner
Usually cotton, sometimes corn — peanuts fix nitrogen for the next crop.
